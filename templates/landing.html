Supply chain diagnostics

Cost and carbon waste the same routes. Nobody looks at both.

Freight cost is managed by one team, in dollars, every week. Freight carbon is reported by another team, in tonnes, once a year. The routes doing both kinds of damage sit in the gap between them, which is exactly why they survive. This tool reads your order data and finds them.

Three steps, in order

  1. 01

    See the chain

    Every order collapses into routes, and the routes into six stages: suppliers, inbound, warehousing, outbound, customers, returns. Most companies have never seen all six on one page.

  2. 02

    Find what is wrong

    Each stage runs its own check. Freight on a mode that is beaten on both cost and carbon. Warehouses on a dirty power grid. Destinations that cost too much to reach. Routes where too much comes back.

  3. 03

    Get the plan

    A numbered list of changes. What to do, what it saves in money, what it saves in carbon, and the operational catch that will decide whether you can actually do it.

What makes it different

A route being expensive is a procurement question. A route being dirty is a reporting question. Neither is interesting alone, and both already have an owner. This only surfaces routes where one change fixes both, because those are the only ones where the business case writes itself.

That distinction is enforced in the maths, not in the marketing. A route is flagged only when a change cuts at least 25 percent of its cost and at least 25 percent of its carbon.

Why the numbers hold up

Emission factors come from the published DEFRA and GLEC ranges, the method most freight carbon reporting is built on. Cost factors are labelled as defaults rather than dressed up as standards. Every formula is written out, along with what the tool does not account for.

There is no model and no training data. It is arithmetic on your own figures, and you can reproduce any number on the page by hand. The method page shows all of it.
