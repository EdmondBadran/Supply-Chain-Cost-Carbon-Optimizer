Why only some routes are flagged

A route being expensive is a procurement question. A route being dirty is a reporting question. Neither is interesting alone, and both already have an owner. This surfaces only routes where one change fixes both, because those are the ones where the business case writes itself.

That is enforced in the arithmetic, not in the copy. A route is flagged only when a single change cuts at least 25 percent of its cost and at least 25 percent of its carbon. On the sample above that leaves {{ report.problems|length }} problems out of {{ "{:,}".format(facts.routes) }} routes.

Why the numbers hold up

Emission factors come from the published DEFRA and GLEC ranges, the method most freight carbon reporting is built on. Cost factors are labelled as defaults rather than dressed up as standards. There is no model and no training data: it is arithmetic on your own figures, and you can reproduce any number on the page by hand.

Because those factors are published as ranges rather than constants, the whole ranking is re-run two thousand times with them redrawn, and the saving is quoted as a band as well as a figure. Every formula is written out, along with what the tool does not account for at all.

What happens to your data

Order files are commercially sensitive. This one never lands on a disk, and no two visitors can see each other's.

  1. It arrives and is read

    Your CSV is held in a temporary file for as long as it takes to parse it, which is under a second, and deleted before the request finishes. That happens whether the file loaded cleanly or not.

  2. It becomes a graph in memory

    The rows turn into locations and routes inside a database that exists only in the server's memory and belongs to your browser session alone. Nothing about it is written to disk, and another visitor loading their own file cannot reach yours or replace it.

  3. Then it is gone

    Two hours without a request and your workspace is dropped. Pressing clear drops it immediately, and restarting the server drops every one of them. There is no database to back up, and nothing to delete later, because nothing was kept.

What that does not mean is that the server cannot read your data. It has to: it is the thing doing the arithmetic. Claiming otherwise would need the whole calculation to run in your browser, which is a different piece of software, and it is not a claim worth making unless it is true. The full version is here, and the hundred lines of code behind it are worth reading rather than believing.

How it is built

1 runtime dependency, and that is Flask
34,135 cities geocoded from a bundled table, so it runs offline
115 tests, standard library, no test dependency either
No build step Python, SQLite and server rendered templates, with D3 for the map. Haversine is registered as a SQLite function, so distance is worked out in the same query that groups the orders.
