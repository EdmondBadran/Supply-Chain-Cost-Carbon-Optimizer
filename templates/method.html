How the numbers are worked out

Every figure this tool shows, where it comes from, and what it does not account for. Nothing here is a black box.

The shape of the calculation

Your CSV becomes a graph. Suppliers, warehouses and customer cities are points. Every shipping route between two points is a lane. Orders going the same way by the same transport mode are grouped into one lane, so a year of deliveries to London becomes a single lane carrying a year of weight.

Everything after that is worked out per lane, then added up. Nothing is estimated at the company level and divided down.

Distance

City names are matched against a bundled GeoNames table of 34,135 cities across 244 countries, which gives a latitude and longitude for each end of the lane. Distance is the great-circle distance between those two points, using the haversine formula on a 6,371 km earth radius.

What this misses: real freight does not travel in straight lines. Road distance typically runs 15 to 25 percent longer than great-circle, and sea routes can be far longer where they route around land masses or through canals. Absolute distances here run low. The comparison between two options on the same lane is unaffected, because both are measured the same way, and that comparison is what the ranking rests on.

Emissions

Freight emissions are measured in kg CO2e per tonne-kilometre, which is the standard unit for the job: one tonne carried one kilometre. A lane's transport emissions are its annual weight in tonnes, times distance in km, times the factor for its mode.

Air is roughly 75 times sea per tonne-km. That single ratio is why air freight dominates the results on any chain that uses it, and why moving a lane off air is almost always the largest carbon win available.

The other two sources of emissions

Packaging is charged per order at {{ packaging_co2e }} kg CO2e, because it scales with how many parcels go out rather than how far they travel.

Warehouse energy is each site's annual kWh times its grid carbon intensity, shared across that site's lanes by weight. If you do not supply a grid intensity, {{ grid_default }} kg CO2e per kWh is assumed, which is roughly the world average. Supplying real figures matters here: the gap between a site on hydro power and one on a coal-heavy grid is larger than most people expect.

Returns are charged as the outbound leg again at {{ return_multiplier }} times, because reverse logistics is less consolidated than the trip out.

Cost to serve

Cost per lane is transport, plus that lane's share of the warehouse it ships from, plus what its returns cost.

These are defaults, not published standards. Road and rail sit in the normal range for contracted freight. Air is derived from general long haul cargo at roughly 2 to 5 USD per kg, which is about 3,000 USD a tonne over a 16,000 km route, or 0.19 per tonne-km.

Treat these as a starting point. Real freight rates vary enormously by contract, lane, volume and season, far more than emission factors do. If you have your own rates, they belong in optimizer/factors.py and the whole cost side of the tool gets sharper for it. The carbon side does not depend on them at all.

How warehouse cost is shared out

A site's annual storage and handling cost is divided across its lanes by weight. A lane carrying a fifth of what leaves a warehouse absorbs a fifth of that warehouse's cost.

Why weight and not orders or revenue: splitting by order count under-charges heavy freight, which uses far more of a building than a small parcel does. Splitting by revenue mixes up cost to serve with margin, which are different questions. Weight is the closest honest proxy for how much of a warehouse a lane actually consumes. It is still a proxy: a pallet of something bulky and light occupies more space than its weight suggests.

Returns

Each returned order is charged the outbound transport again at {{ return_multiplier }} times, plus {{ return_handling }} USD of handling.

What counts as a problem

A lane being expensive is a procurement question. A lane being dirty is a reporting question. Neither is what this tool is for. It flags lanes where changing the transport mode cuts both cost and carbon by at least {{ "{:.0%}".format(flag_threshold) }} each.

Being large is not enough on its own. A lane already running the best mode available to it has nothing to recover, so it never appears no matter how big the number beside it is. The ranking runs on what a change would give back, not on the size of the problem.

Which mode changes are offered

Without real routing data, the safe assumption is that a lane keeps reaching its destination the way it already does.

  • Air can drop to sea beyond {{ "{:,}".format(sea_minimum) }} km, or to road and rail under {{ "{:,}".format(surface_range) }} km
  • Road can move to rail at any distance, since it is already a land route
  • Sea and rail are left alone. Nothing beats sea per tonne-km

These gates are deliberately conservative. A suggestion the tool misses is invisible. A suggestion that is obviously impossible, like shipping freight overland between two inland cities by sea, makes every other number on the page look unreliable. The tool would rather stay quiet than be wrong out loud.

It also does not know your business. Air freight is often there for a reason: perishable goods, urgent replacements, contracts with delivery windows sea cannot meet. That is exactly why the tool asks you to tag how hard each change would be rather than assuming. Tagging something hard weights it at {{ effort_weights['high'] }} and drops it down the list; tagging it easy weights it at {{ effort_weights['low'] }} and lifts it up.

Problems found in each stage

The value chain view groups the same data by where in the business it sits, and applies a check appropriate to that stage.

  • Suppliers and freight: lanes flagged by the mode-switch test above
  • Warehousing: sites emitting at least 40 percent more carbon per tonne handled than the median of your other sites, which usually means a dirty grid rather than a badly run building
  • Customers: destinations costing at least 50 percent more per tonne to serve than the median destination
  • Returns: lanes where more than 8 percent of orders come back

Medians are used rather than averages throughout, so one extreme site or destination cannot drag the comparison line with it.

What this tool does not do

  • It does not model transit time, so a change that saves money and carbon may still be unacceptable on lead time
  • It does not model inventory. Slower shipping means more stock in transit and more working capital tied up, and that cost is not counted here
  • It does not model capacity. Moving volume to a different warehouse assumes that warehouse can take it
  • It covers freight, storage, packaging and returns. It is not a full product carbon footprint and says nothing about manufacturing, raw materials or end of life
  • Supplier terms beyond freight, meaning lead time, minimum order quantity and on-time rate, are not modelled yet

Everything above is arithmetic on figures you can check. There is no model, no training data and no estimation you cannot reproduce by hand from your own CSV and the factors on this page.
