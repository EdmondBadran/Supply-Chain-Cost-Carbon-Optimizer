How the numbers are worked out

Every figure this tool shows, where it comes from, and what it does not account for. Nothing here is a black box.

The shape of the calculation

Your CSV becomes a network. Suppliers, warehouses and customer cities are points on it. Orders travelling between the same two points by the same transport mode are grouped into one route, so a year of deliveries to London becomes a single route carrying a year of weight.

Everything after that is worked out per route, then added up. Nothing is estimated at company level and divided down.

The freight industry calls a route between two points a lane, and you will see that word in the code and in any carrier contract. This site says "route" throughout, because it means the same thing and needs less explaining.

Distance

City names are matched against a bundled GeoNames table of 34,135 cities across 244 countries, which gives a latitude and longitude for each end of the route. Distance is the great-circle distance between those two points, using the haversine formula on a 6,371 km earth radius.

What this misses: real freight does not travel in straight lines, and how far it strays depends on the mode. Aircraft fly close to great-circle. Road runs about 25 percent longer, rail about 40 percent, and a sea route around a land mass or through a canal can be twice the straight line: Shanghai to Rotterdam is about 9,200 km direct and about 19,500 km by ship. Measuring every mode as a straight line therefore understates the slower modes more than the faster ones, and those are the modes this tool recommends switching to. The direction of a recommendation is rarely affected, because sea and rail win by so much per tonne-km, but the size of the saving is optimistic. Treat the figures as an upper bound on what a switch is worth.

Emissions

Freight emissions are measured in kg CO2e per tonne-kilometre, which is the standard unit for the job: one tonne carried one kilometre. A route's transport emissions are its annual weight in tonnes, times its distance in km, times the emission factor for its mode.

Source: the ranges published by the UK Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (DEFRA) in its greenhouse gas conversion factors, and the Global Logistics Emissions Council (GLEC) framework, which is the method most freight carbon reporting is built on.

Air is roughly 75 times sea per tonne-km. That single ratio is why air freight dominates the results on any chain that uses it, and why moving a route off air freight is almost always the largest carbon saving available.

The other two sources of emissions

Packaging is charged per order at {{ packaging_co2e }} kg CO2e, because it scales with how many parcels go out rather than how far they travel.

Warehouse energy is each site's annual kWh times its grid carbon intensity, shared across that site's routes by weight. If you do not supply a grid intensity, {{ grid_default }} kg CO2e per kWh is assumed, which is roughly the world average. Supplying real figures matters here: the gap between a site on hydro power and one on a coal-heavy grid is larger than most people expect.

Returns are charged as the outbound leg again at {{ return_multiplier }} times, because reverse logistics is less consolidated than the trip out.

Cost to serve

Cost per route is its transport cost, plus its share of the warehouse it ships from, plus what its returns cost.

ModeUSD per tonne-km
{{ mode }}{{ value }}

These are defaults, not published standards. Road and rail sit in the normal range for contracted freight. Air is derived from general long haul cargo at roughly 2 to 5 USD per kg, which is about 3,000 USD a tonne over a 16,000 km route, or 0.19 per tonne-km.

Treat these as a starting point. Real freight rates vary enormously by contract, route, volume and season, far more than emission factors do. If you have your own rates, they belong in optimizer/factors.py and the whole cost side of the tool gets sharper for it. The carbon side does not depend on them at all.

How warehouse cost is shared out

A site's annual storage and handling cost is divided across its routes by weight. A route carrying a fifth of everything that leaves a warehouse absorbs a fifth of that warehouse's cost.

Why weight and not orders or revenue: splitting by order count under-charges heavy freight, which uses far more of a building than a small parcel does. Splitting by revenue mixes up cost to serve with margin, which are different questions. Weight is the closest honest proxy for how much of a warehouse a route actually uses. It is still a proxy: a pallet of something bulky and light occupies more space than its weight suggests.

Packaging

Charged per order, not per tonne-km, at ${{ "%.2f"|format(packaging_cost) }} and {{ packaging_co2e }} kg CO2e each. A parcel needs the same box whether it travels 20 km or 2,000, so this is the one figure in the tool that distance does not touch. It is counted in the warehousing stage on both the cost and the carbon side.

Returns

Each returned order is charged the outbound transport again at {{ return_multiplier }} times, plus ${{ "%.2f"|format(return_handling) }} of handling.

Transit time

A mode switch is only useful if you know what it does to lead time, so each route also gets a rough door-to-door estimate: distance divided by a typical speed for the mode, plus a fixed allowance at each end for collection, customs, terminal or port handling and final delivery.

Modekm per dayFixed days
{{ mode }} {{ "{:,}".format(transit_speed[mode]) }} {{ transit_fixed[mode] }}

These are planning figures, not schedules. Road is capped by driver hours rather than vehicle speed, rail loses hours in terminals and marshalling, and a container ship cruising at about 20 knots still spends days in port at each end. They are the right order of magnitude and nothing more. Use them to tell weeks from days, not to promise a date.

What counts as a problem

An expensive route is a procurement question. A high-carbon route is a reporting question. Neither on its own is what this tool is for. It flags only routes where changing the transport mode cuts both cost and carbon by at least {{ "{:.0%}".format(flag_threshold) }} each.

Being large is not enough on its own. A route already running the best mode available to it has nothing to recover, so it never appears however big it is. The ranking is based on what a change would save, not on the size of the route.

Which mode changes are offered

Without real routing data, the safe assumption is that a route keeps reaching its destination the way it already does.

  • Air can drop to sea beyond {{ "{:,}".format(sea_minimum) }} km, or to road and rail under {{ "{:,}".format(surface_range) }} km
  • Road can move to rail at any distance, since it is already a land route
  • Sea and rail are left alone. Nothing beats sea per tonne-km

These gates are deliberately conservative. A suggestion the tool misses is invisible. A suggestion that is obviously impossible, like shipping freight overland between two inland cities by sea, makes every other number on the page look unreliable. The tool would rather stay quiet than be wrong out loud.

It also does not know your business. Air freight is often there for a reason: perishable goods, urgent replacements, contracts with delivery windows sea cannot meet. That is exactly why the tool asks you to tag how hard each change would be rather than assuming. Tagging something hard weights it at {{ effort_weights['high'] }} and drops it down the list; tagging it easy weights it at {{ effort_weights['low'] }} and lifts it up.

Problems found in each stage

The value chain view groups the same data by where in the business it sits, and applies a check appropriate to that stage.

  • Inbound and outbound freight: routes flagged by the mode-switch test above
  • Suppliers: the same flagged routes are named again here, because the mode is something you negotiate with a supplier. The money stays with the freight stage, so it is only counted once
  • Warehousing: sites emitting at least 40 percent more carbon per tonne handled than the median of your other sites, which usually means a carbon-intensive electricity grid rather than a badly run building
  • Customers: destinations costing at least 50 percent more per tonne to serve than the median destination. Routes already flagged for a mode switch are excluded, because there the cost is coming from the mode rather than from the distance to the customer
  • Returns: routes where more than 8 percent of orders come back

Medians are used rather than averages throughout, so one extreme site or destination cannot drag the comparison line with it.

A route can fail more than one check. When it does, the ranked list in step 2 keeps it once, under whichever problem is worth most, and offers the other as an alternative fix. The per-stage figures in step 1 are not meant to be added together for that reason. The de-duplicated total is the recoverable figure quoted at the top.

Supplier terms

Lead time, minimum order quantity and on-time rate are commercial terms rather than freight, so they are only counted when a supplier file supplies them. Two of the three carry no money here, and the page says so rather than inventing a figure.

  • On-time rate below {{ "{:.0%}".format(on_time_target) }}. This is the one with money on it. A missed date gets recovered by expediting, and expediting means air, so {{ "{:.0%}".format(expedite_share) }} of whatever runs late is assumed to fly. The cost and carbon shown are the difference between the lane's normal mode and air freight for that share only. A supplier already shipping by air raises nothing, because there is nothing faster to escalate to
  • Lead time over {{ lead_time_limit }} days. Flagged, not costed: past this you are ordering against a demand forecast rather than a schedule, and what that is worth depends on your demand, which is not in an orders file
  • Minimum order quantity covering more than {{ "{:,.0f}".format(moq_months) }} months of demand. Flagged, not costed: it ties up working capital at a rate this tool does not know

The {{ "{:.0%}".format(expedite_share) }} expedite assumption is the single softest number in this tool. It is a planning figure, not a measurement. Halve it and the supplier findings roughly halve with it, so if you have your own expedite records, use those instead.

Closing and opening warehouses

The map's network panel reassigns work when a site closes or a new one opens. Only routes actually affected move: a route whose site is still open, and still the nearest, stays exactly where it is. Changing nothing therefore saves exactly nothing, which is the only way the rest of the answer can be trusted.

  • A closed site's routes go to the nearest remaining site with room, heaviest routes first
  • A site with a capacity_kg figure in the warehouse file is held to it. Without one, it is assumed to absorb up to {{ "{:.0%}".format(capacity_headroom - 1) }} more than the volume it handles today. Either way the overflow goes to the next nearest site, and if nothing has room the page says the shape is over capacity rather than pretending otherwise. Each site is labelled stated or assumed so the two are never read as the same thing
  • Closing a site removes its storage cost and its electricity, which is usually most of the reason closing one looks attractive
  • A new site is priced at the median cost and energy per tonne of your existing sites, on the world average grid intensity of {{ grid_default }} kg CO2e per kWh, because nothing in your data describes a building that does not exist yet

The statistics on top of the arithmetic

Everything above turns your file into figures. The last step of the report turns those figures back on themselves and asks how much weight they will take. Five standard methods, each chosen because the obvious alternative fails on data this shape.

Gini coefficient and the Lorenz curve
How unevenly cost and carbon are shared across routes. Zero is every route carrying the same amount, one is a single route carrying all of it. It answers whether the fix list is four conversations or a programme.
Spearman rank correlation
Whether the expensive routes and the high-carbon routes are the same routes, which is the assumption this whole tool rests on. Ranks rather than Pearson on the raw values, because route sizes are heavy-tailed and one enormous route would otherwise decide the answer on its own.
Monte Carlo over the factors
The ranking re-run {{ "{:,}".format(trials) }} times with every cost and emission factor redrawn from a triangular distribution up to {{ "{:.0%}".format(factor_spread) }} either side of the published value. The factors are drawn once per run and applied to every route together, because if fuel is dearer than assumed it is dearer everywhere at once. Drawing them per route would let the errors cancel and give a band far narrower than the truth.
Median absolute deviation
Finding routes priced unlike the rest. A standard deviation is no use with a few dozen routes: one genuine outlier inflates the spread it is being measured against until it stops looking unusual. The median does not move.
Wilson score interval
Return rates with the uncertainty attached. Below about thirty observations the textbook interval produces bounds under zero, and most routes in a small network sit in exactly that range. A route is only called worse than the network when its whole interval is above the network rate.

None of this changes a recommendation. It tells you how hard to lean on one, which is a different and more useful thing than a confidence score attached to the answer itself.

What this tool does not do

  • Transit time is estimated from typical door-to-door speeds per mode, not quoted by a carrier. It is good enough to tell you a change adds weeks rather than days, and not good enough to plan against
  • It does not model inventory. Slower shipping means more stock in transit and more working capital tied up, and that cost is not counted here
  • Where no capacity is given, one headroom figure stands in for racking, doors, labour and licence, none of which an orders file knows about. Treat an over-capacity warning on an assumed limit as a prompt to check rather than a measurement, and give the warehouse file a capacity_kg column if you want the answer to mean something
  • It covers freight, storage, packaging and returns. It is not a full product carbon footprint and says nothing about manufacturing, raw materials or end of life
  • Supplier terms are only as good as the terms you supply. Without a lead time, a minimum order or an on-time rate in the supplier file, none of those checks run at all, and the supplier stage falls back to freight alone

Everything above is arithmetic on figures you can check. There is no model, no training data and no estimation you cannot reproduce by hand from your own CSV and the factors on this page.
